    Manila, 1849

    Catálogo alfabético de apellidos

    About 60,000 surnames, printed and sent to every province, for families who had none. Many surnames Filipinos carry today are in these pages.

    Sources: the decree, Arts. 1–3, 1973 reprint, pp. x–xi; today's surnames: Apelyido's data

    1 Before 1849

    A country of namesakes.

    By 1849 thousands of people shared one saint's name. A man was called de la Cruz, and so were his neighbours, and his children might take another name altogether. Few families had a surname that passed from parent to child. The tax rolls and the parish books could not say who was who.

    Source: the decree, preamble and Art. 9, 1973 reprint, pp. x–xi

    2 The decree

    One book, one order.

    Governor-General Narciso Clavería answered with a list. On 21 November 1849 he ordered a catalogue of surnames, Spanish names, native words, plants, places, printed and sent to every province, and every family without a fixed surname was to take one from it.

    Source: the decree, Arts. 1–3, 1973 reprint, pp. x–xi

    3 In the town

    A name for every household.

    Each town received its share of the pages. The parish priest and the town's officials saw that every head of family chose a surname from them, or was given one, and it passed to his descendants. Families who already had a surname kept it. Four were off limits, Lacandola, Mójica, Tupas and Raja Matanda, reserved for those with a proven title to them.

    Sources: the decree, Arts. 2–4 and 8, 1973 reprint, pp. x–xi; Abella, introduction, pp. vii–viii

    4 Letter by letter

    Albay, letter by letter.

    In old Albay the alphabet was handed out town by town: A at the capital, B and C along the coast to Tiwi, and so on round the province. By 1895 a historian noted towns where almost every surname began with A, others with B.

    Sources: Abella, introduction, 1973 reprint, p. vii; Montero y Vidal, quoted by Abella, p. xiv

    5 Afterwards

    What it did, and didn't.

    It was widely enforced in Camarines and Tayabas, and virtually ignored in parts of Laguna. Its real aim, a complete tax list, “failed to a great extent,” wrote Domingo Abella, who edited the 1973 reprint. The names outlived the purpose.

    Source: Abella, introduction, 1973 reprint, pp. xiii–xv

    Names on these pages

    Catálogo alfabético de apellidos

    In 1849, many Filipinos had a baptismal name but no family name passed down, and so many shared the same saints' names that the tax lists and parish books couldn't tell people apart (the decree's preamble, 1973 reprint, p. x). So Governor-General Narciso Clavería's decree of 21 November 1849 sent this book of about 60,000 names, Spanish and native, to every province, for families without a fixed surname to take one.

    Every page is set in type from an open, machine-read transcription of the National Archives of the Philippines' 1973 reprint, read from three scans: Google's, of the University of Michigan's copy, the Filipinas Heritage Library's, and FamilySearch's microfilm. Each name keeps its column and line from the scanned page. Some readings are uncertain.
