    The fix

    60,000

    One book of surnames, sent to every province.

    Spanish names, native words, plants, places. On 21 November 1849 Governor-General Narciso Clavería ordered families without a fixed surname to take one from it.

    Source: Clavería's decree, Arts. 1–3, 1973 reprint, pp. x–xi

    Letter by letter

    A · B · C · D

    In Albay, the alphabet went town by town.

    Each town was given a letter of the book. By 1895 a historian noted towns where almost every surname began with A, others with B.

    Sources: Abella, introduction, 1973 reprint, p. vii; Montero y Vidal, quoted by Abella, p. xiv

    Did it work?

    Not really. But the names stayed.

    It was enforced hard in some provinces and almost ignored in others, and its main aim, a complete tax list, “failed to a great extent,” says Domingo Abella, who edited the 1973 reprint.

    Source: Abella, introduction, 1973 reprint, pp. xiii–xv

    Catálogo alfabético de apellidos

    In 1849, many Filipinos had a baptismal name but no family name passed down, and so many shared the same saints' names that the tax lists and parish books couldn't tell people apart (the decree's preamble, 1973 reprint, p. x). So Governor-General Narciso Clavería's decree of 21 November 1849 sent this book of about 60,000 names, Spanish and native, to every province, for families without a fixed surname to take one.

    Every page is set in type from an open, machine-read transcription of the National Archives of the Philippines' 1973 reprint, read from two scans: Google's, of the University of Michigan's copy, and the Filipinas Heritage Library's. Each name keeps its column and line from the scanned page. Some readings are uncertain.
